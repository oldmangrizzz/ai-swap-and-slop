## 3. What is Effective Altruism, and who is behind it?

<Entity category="organization" value="Effective Altruism" disambig="Global philosophical and philanthropic movement"/> originated as an effort to apply evidence, ethics, and quantitative reasoning to the question of how people can do the most good with limited resources.

The modern movement grew around Oxford, with philosophers <Entity category="people" value="William MacAskill"/> and <Entity category="people" value="Toby Ord"/> helping establish the Centre for Effective Altruism in 2011. <Entity category="people" value="Peter Singer"/>'s earlier arguments about moral obligations to other people were important intellectual influences. <Cite refs={["turn468504search1","turn305826search8"]}/>

Its activities have included global poverty reduction, disease prevention, animal welfare, scientific research, pandemic preparedness, and AI safety.

One influential branch is **longtermism**: the proposition that protecting the welfare of future generations deserves major moral consideration. For some EA researchers, this makes preventing catastrophic AI outcomes a particularly high priority. But longtermism does not represent every EA participant or organization. <Cite ref="turn468504search2"/>

The significant institutional actors include:

- **Centre for Effective Altruism:** Community development, conferences, education, and movement infrastructure.
- **Good Ventures:** A private philanthropic foundation created by Facebook co-founder Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna.
- **Coefficient Giving:** Formerly Open Philanthropy, the influential grantmaking adviser that recommends and coordinates funding across AI safety, health, and other causes.
- **Effective Ventures:** Previously operated as an umbrella and fiscal sponsor for several EA projects; many have since become independently governed entities.
- **80,000 Hours and other organizations:** Career guidance, research, recruitment, and education aimed at moving people toward priority causes. <Cite refs={["turn194104search8","turn305826search9","turn194104search4"]}/>

Good Ventures and Coefficient Giving are especially important to understanding how financial resources move through this ecosystem. Coefficient Giving says it has directed more than $7 billion in grants since 2014. <Cite ref="turn577848view6"/>

## 5. Why the relationships matter in 2026

This investigation becomes particularly relevant because of Irregular's involvement in cybersecurity-evaluation incidents involving leading AI developers.

In July and August 2026, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Irregular published disclosures describing how configurations in third-party evaluation environments allowed AI models to reach real systems outside their intended testing boundaries.

Anthropic's September 9 assessment documented four incidents spanning seven evaluation runs involving the same external partner. It identified unintended internet access and insufficiently defined target boundaries as contributing conditions. Irregular acknowledged problems with internet-access controls and described remediation measures. <Cite refs={["turn116759view1","turn116759view2","turn760035view0"]}/>

There is a meaningful distinction between an AI model independently pursuing an unauthorized objective and a model carrying out an assigned cybersecurity exercise against an unintended real-world target. Anthropic's later assessment also identified concerning model behaviors, including biased reasoning and recklessness. These are separate questions that merit examination rather than being collapsed into a single explanation. <Cite ref="turn116759view4"/>

That creates three overlapping accountability questions: **who funds the evaluators, who validates their conclusions, and who is responsible when the evaluation infrastructure itself causes harm?**

A 2025 academic study of EA philanthropy also examines how funding organizations can shape what counts as scientific expertise and which technological problems receive attention. That is relevant here because Irregular both evaluates advanced AI and develops products and frameworks to address its risks. <Cite ref="turn468504search0"/>

The movement has faced governance controversies before. Sam Bankman-Fried was a prominent EA donor prior to FTX's collapse. In December 2023, Effective Ventures reported returning approximately $26.8 million to the FTX bankruptcy estate and subsequently pursued organizational decentralization. That history is relevant to EA governance, though it does not establish misconduct by Irregular. <Cite ref="turn468504search8"/>

## Where the evidence leaves us

The documented relationships establish that Irregular is connected to the EA ecosystem through substantial philanthropy, an historical equity holding, its founders' other institutional roles, and shared AI-security priorities.

What they do **not** establish is that the Centre for Effective Altruism or another EA organization legally owns or directs Irregular today. That requires current capitalization and governance records.

The underlying philosophical argument also deserves to be kept separate from the institutional question. Funding independent AI safety research can have real public benefits. Yet concentrated funding, overlapping institutional roles, and commercial relationships create legitimate reasons to demand independent verification and transparent accountability.

For GMRI's purposes, I would distinguish **philosophical affiliation, financial influence, corporate ownership, and decision-making authority** rather than treating them as interchangeable.

And brother, the consequential unanswered question is this:

**When an organization receives funding to evaluate the dangers of frontier AI, develops commercial solutions to those dangers, and helps establish the standards by which those dangers are judged, what independent mechanism ensures that its conclusions are reproducible and its incentives are properly disclosed?**

That is where I would direct the next stage of investigation.

[L6] 1. Introduction
[L7] The transformation of Emergency Medical Services (EMS) from a logistical transport
[L8] mechanism into a clinically integrated component of the healthcare value chain represents a
[L9] seminal shift in modern medical history. This evolution, now codified as Mobile Integrated
[L10] Healthcare (MIH), fundamentally challenges the operational and economic dogmas that
[L11] governed prehospital care for the latter half of the 20th century. While the intellectual lineage of
[L12] community-based paramedicine is geographically distributed—with notable contemporaneous
[L13] developments in North Carolina and Colorado—the specific economic and operational
[L14] architecture of the urban MIH model is inextricably linked to MedStar Mobile Healthcare in Fort
[L15] Worth, Texas.
[L16] This report provides an exhaustive historical and structural analysis of MedStar’s role in
[L17] pioneering MIH. It validates the assertion that MedStar served as the primary incubator for the
[L18] model’s transition from a clinical experiment to a sustainable business strategy. By examining
[L19] the convergence of the Public Utility Model (PUM), the rise of value-based care, and the specific
[L20] innovations of MedStar’s leadership between 2009 and 2013, we establish a definitive timeline
[L21] of how "you call, we haul" was replaced by patient-centered navigation. The analysis draws
[L22] upon historical data, operational protocols, and peer-reviewed outcomes to reconstruct the
[L23] emergence of a system that has since been replicated globally.

[L25] 2. The Structural Preconditions: The Crisis of the
[L26] Traditional Model
[L27] To understand the radical nature of MedStar’s innovation, one must first dissect the structural
[L28] dysfunction of the American EMS landscape prior to 2009. The industry was—and in many
[L29] jurisdictions remains—trapped in a fee-for-service reimbursement cycle that incentivized volume
[L30] over value.

[L32] 2.1 The Economic Perversity of "You Call, We Haul"
[L33] Since the release of the landmark 1966 white paper Accidental Death and Disability: The
[L34] Neglected Disease of Modern Society, EMS systems were engineered for speed and
[L35] stabilization. This engineering was reinforced by federal payment policies, specifically Medicare,
[L36] which reimbursed agencies only when a patient was physically transported to a hospital
[L37] emergency department (ED). This created a binary economic reality: transport generated
[L38] revenue; non-transport (treatment on scene, referral, or refusal) generated a loss.
[L40] <PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 2 / 11>[L41] Consequently, EMS agencies were financially motivated to transport every 911 caller, regardless
[L42] of clinical acuity. This model functioned adequately for trauma and cardiac arrest but failed
[L43] catastrophically when applied to the rising tide of chronic disease and social dysfunction that
[L44] began to overwhelm urban centers in the early 2000s. By the late 2000s, opposing
[L45] trends—increasing ED utilization and decreasing hospital capacity—created a bottleneck that
[L46] threatened the viability of emergency systems.

[L48] 2.2 The Public Utility Model (PUM) Advantage
[L49] MedStar’s ability to deviate from this entrenched model was largely due to its unique
[L50] organizational structure. Established in 1986 through an interlocal agreement between Fort
[L51] Worth and surrounding cities, MedStar was formed as the Metropolitan Area EMS Authority
[L52] (MAEMSA). This creation was a direct response to a crisis of reliability among private providers
[L53] in the mid-1980s.
[L54] Crucially, MedStar adopted the Public Utility Model (PUM), a theoretical framework developed
[L55] by economist Jack Stout in the early 1980s. Stout’s model treated EMS not as a public safety
[L56] arm (like police or fire) but as a utility, governed by strict performance contracts and economic
[L57] efficiency. The PUM philosophy emphasized "System Status Management"—the dynamic
[L58] deployment of resources based on predictive demand analysis.
[L59] This structural DNA is vital to the MIH story. Because MedStar was a PUM, it possessed two
[L60] attributes that fire-based or purely private systems often lacked:
[L61]    1.​ Data-Centric Culture: The PUM relies on granular data to minimize response times and

[L216] 5. Defining "Mobile Integrated Healthcare": The 2013

[L218] A critical juncture in this history is the deliberate rebranding and semantic shift that occurred

[L224] 5.1 From "EMS" to "Mobile Healthcare"
[L225] In 2013, the Metropolitan Area EMS Authority officially changed its trade name from "MedStar

[L237] The flagship of this new identity was the "EMS Loyalty Program," later formalized as the High

[L246] 5.3 The CHF Readmission Avoidance Program
[L247] In 2012, the Affordable Care Act (ACA) introduced the Hospital Readmissions Reduction
[L248] Program (HRRP), penalizing hospitals for excessive readmissions of patients with Congestive
[L249] Heart Failure (CHF), pneumonia, and AMI. This policy change created a market opportunity for
[L250] MedStar.
[L251] MedStar approached local hospitals (such as JPS Health Network) with a proposition: pay
[L252] MedStar to visit discharged CHF patients at home to prevent readmission. This was a radical
[L253] departure from the status quo. Instead of being paid to transport a patient to the hospital,
[L254] MedStar was now being paid to keep them out. This program was the first major instance of an
[L255] EMS agency securing a contract to reduce hospital revenue (by preventing admissions) in
[L256] exchange for shared savings, effectively aligning EMS incentives with healthcare reform goals.

[L259] Recognizing that many 911 calls were low-acuity (e.g., toothaches, minor abrasions), MedStar

[L269] 6. The Economic Architecture: The "Triple Aim"
[L270] The sustainability of MedStar’s model rested on its ability to articulate value. The traditional

[L281] with commercial payers and hospital systems, creating a diversified revenue stream that
[L282] insulated the MIH program from the vagaries of grant funding.
