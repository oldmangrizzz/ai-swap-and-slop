# 1. A City That Never Asked

The bus came into Gotham at 3:17 in the morning, which was an hour when most cities had the decency to look asleep.

Gotham looked awake and angry about it.

Rain dragged silver lines through the terminal windows. Above the bus bays, a huge electronic billboard advertised a smiling family under the words **A SAFER CITY STARTS WITH US**. Every third letter flickered. Beyond it stood buildings so tall their upper floors disappeared into cloud. A clock tower marked the hour without making a sound.

Jack Reacher stepped down carrying a toothbrush, a folded piece of paper, and nothing else.

The paper had come from Frances Neagley. Reacher hadn't seen her for eight months, but she could still find him when it mattered. She had left a message through the peculiar network of bank contacts, motel clerks, and stubborn old soldiers that passed for his address.

*Mara Rusk. Gotham. Missing four days. June asked for you.*

Beneath that were six numbers for a telephone contact and one sentence in Neagley's handwriting.

*Something is wrong with the police reports.*

Reacher had known Mara's father in the Army. David Rusk was a sergeant with the sort of practical intelligence that kept a unit alive without ever attracting a medal. Once, in a country whose border signs had changed twice in a year, Rusk had pulled Reacher out of a burning vehicle. Reacher had thanked him. Rusk had told him to buy the beer.

David had died two years ago, peacefully and without asking permission, which Reacher considered an uncharacteristic lapse in judgment.

Mara was twenty-nine. An electrical engineer. Too smart to be easily lost, according to her mother. June Rusk had spent the previous four days being told that adults were allowed to disappear.

Reacher agreed with that principle. He simply didn't like how many people had been eager to explain it to her.

Outside the terminal, a taxi driver glanced at his size and decided to be busy elsewhere. Reacher walked across the wet street to a diner whose sign read ALL NIGHT, although half the letters had surrendered.

Inside, the waitress had gray hair pinned with two pencils and the flat, alert eyes of a woman who had worked behind a counter when bars emptied.

"Coffee?" she asked.

"Yes. Food too."

"What food?"

"What have you got?"

"That depends how brave you are."

"Eggs. Six. Bacon. Toast."

She looked at him for a moment, then called the order through a hatch. "You new here?"

"Passing through."

"Nobody passes through Gotham. They come in or they get stuck."

"I'll make a note."

She delivered coffee that could have stripped paint. Reacher drank it and watched the street in the dark reflection of a window. A delivery van rolled past slowly, black except for a white mark on the sliding door: three vertical bars connected by a crosspiece. An ornate capital I, perhaps. The van was too clean for the hour and the weather. Two people occupied the front. The passenger wasn't looking for addresses. He was looking at windows.

A city could have a hundred explanations for that. Reacher filed it under interesting.

He found a pay phone beside the bathrooms. It worked, which in Gotham felt like a deliberate trick. June Rusk answered on the fourth ring.

"Mrs. Rusk. It's Jack."

A breath caught on the line. "You came."

"Tell me what happened."

"Mara called last Thursday. She sounded frightened. She told me that if anybody asked, I hadn't heard from her for a month. Then she said something about a bell. I thought she meant a church. She said she was sorry."

"Sorry for what?"

"She didn't say."

"Who employed her?"

"The city, I thought. A contractor. Halcyon Civic. She said it was emergency planning. Getting people out during floods and fires."

"Who filed the missing-person report?"

"I did. They told me she bought a ticket to Metropolis. They said she had a right to go. But the ticket was bought after she stopped using her bank card. And the detective wouldn't tell me who identified her on the station camera."

Reacher considered that. "Did she have a habit of buying tickets she didn't use?"

"No. She had a habit of correcting people who wrote the wrong number on a form."

That sounded like David Rusk's daughter.

"You don't need to come here," June said. "I don't want to put you in danger."

"Good. Neither do I."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

He promised to call again when he had something useful. He didn't promise when. Promises based on guesses were usually worthless.

The waitress brought his breakfast. Six eggs covered most of the plate. She watched him unfold Neagley's note.

"You got family here?" she asked.

"A friend's daughter."

"You don't look like the kind to visit."

"I'm not."

"Then she must be in trouble."

Reacher glanced up. She wasn't curious. She was measuring whether he would bring trouble through her door.

"Maybe."

She took his cup. "There was a girl asking about the old ferry station three nights ago. Brown coat. Hair cut short like she did it herself. Asked where the bells were."

"What did you tell her?"

"That they haven't rung for thirty years."

"Did she believe you?"

"She looked relieved." The waitress hesitated. "Then two men came in asking the same thing. They didn't order."

"And?"

"I told them they hadn't rung for thirty years. That's all I know."

Reacher folded the note. "You know where the old station is?"

She wrote an address on the back of his receipt. The pen made a scratchy sound like a match striking.

"And don't tell anyone I sent you," she said.

"You didn't. I came for the eggs."

The first real smile he had seen in Gotham crossed her face.

By dawn Reacher had found a room above a shuttered tailor shop. The manager wanted cash and a first name. Reacher paid and supplied one. Then he lay on a bed too short by six inches and listened to the city switching shifts.

Somewhere distant, a siren began. Halfway through its warning, the sound bent into a strange, low note. For an instant Reacher remembered the burning vehicle and David Rusk's hand closing around his collar.

He sat up.

The note vanished. The memory remained.

Outside, three gulls lifted from a rooftop in sudden, frantic flight.

Reacher put on his boots.

Whatever Mara Rusk had found, it was awake too.

# 2. The Crime That Made No Noise

From the roof of a grain elevator, Batman watched police lights move through the industrial district like a slow red tide.

The warehouse below had burned from the inside out. Not hot enough to melt the steel doors. Hot enough to turn the security monitors into glassy black puddles. The fire had made no smoke until firefighters arrived.

It was an impressive trick. Batman hated impressive tricks performed in occupied buildings.

He descended through the open ventilation roof and landed on a floor slick with condensation. A stolen freight manifest lay underneath a boot print. The boot was military-pattern, size fourteen, left heel worn unevenly. Most of Gotham's criminals wore expensive sneakers and an exaggerated sense of immunity. This one had been in a hurry.

The manifest listed flood-warning components, speaker assemblies, and emergency power relays, all purchased through Halcyon Civic Solutions. Destination: the Bellhouse, an abandoned municipal pumping and signal station on the East Channel.

One crate was missing. Its lid had been split from within.

In the center of the floor rested a ring of dark material the size of a dinner plate. It looked metallic until his light struck it, whereupon the surface seemed to deepen instead of reflect. Fine marks traveled around its edge. Not letters. Commands, or something shaped like commands.

Batman had seen a cousin of those marks on a weapon taken from an invader who believed Earth was a bargaining chip.

Apokolips.

The word changed the temperature of the room.

His scanner gave three incompatible readings and shut itself down. The ring emitted a soft chord, almost too low to hear. Something tightened behind Batman's eyes.

There was an alley. A boy wearing a red helmet. The impression of a decision made too late.

He gripped the nearest beam until the image passed.

"Master Bruce?" Alfred said in his ear. His voice came through in a wash of static. "You disappeared for twenty-three seconds."

"Found something."

"I gathered that from the complete absence of an explanation."

"Alien technology. Modified. Someone wired it into municipal warning equipment."

There was a pause. Alfred could make pauses sound skeptical.

"You have had rather poor experiences with both of those categories."

"Send me the old city plans. And find everything on Halcyon Civic."

"Already doing so. I took the liberty of considering the possibility you might ask me to think."

Batman lifted a small insulated case from his belt. Inside lay a synthetic cradle intended for materials that didn't obey normal laws. He had built it after a disastrous encounter with a relic beneath Gotham Harbor. He had hoped never to use it again.

The ring moved before he touched it.

Not far. Less than an inch. But it leaned toward his gloved hand as a compass leans toward north.

He closed the case over it.

An alarm pulsed through his cowl. For a moment the warehouse walls seemed farther apart. The floor carried a vibration like a heartbeat belonging to a much larger animal.

Then silence.

"Alfred."

"Still here."

"Find who signed the acceptance certificate."

Paper shuffled at the other end. "Mara Rusk. Contract engineer. Prior service as a civilian electrical specialist with the Army Corps. Missing, according to a report filed four days ago."

"And the project?"

"Officially a system for directing civilians during emergencies. Unofficially, Halcyon has routed a frankly absurd quantity of money through three shell companies with connections to Intergang."

Batman looked at the open crate. "Mannheim."

"The very unpleasant gentleman from Metropolis?"

"He's here."

"I fear Gotham is losing its reputation for exclusivity."

Batman moved toward a service door. On a scorched worktable he found a child's school drawing under a weight. Blue water, a crooked ferry, and a lighthouse with six yellow rays. Someone had circled the lighthouse in red pencil and written *DO NOT RING THE THIRD*.

The drawing wasn't childish. It was an adult copying a child's style to make something look disposable.

He photographed it, then inspected the underside of the table. A slim strip of ordinary masking tape held a second note in the same hand.

*You don't get to save the city by sacrificing the people inside the machine.*

Batman read it twice.

In the distance, police radios called for additional units. A pair of headlights crossed the boarded windows. The warehouse wasn't supposed to be accessible to the police yet. The fire inspector had been ordered to wait.

That meant someone was closing doors.

Batman exited through the roof and looked down at the arriving sedan. A woman stepped out wearing a coat over a white shirt and a badge on her belt. Detective Renee Montoya. She surveyed the damage with a practiced look of disgust.

A uniformed officer spoke to her. She answered without turning. Batman couldn't hear the words, but he could read her mouth.

*No. You're not calling this electrical.*

He almost smiled.

"Alfred," he said. "Find out who assigned Montoya to the fire."

"And the young engineer?"

Batman glanced at the insulated case in his hand. It was vibrating against his palm.

"Find her before Mannheim does."

"You think she's a victim?"

"I think she's in the middle."

That was not the same thing.

He disappeared over the roofline as a second unmarked car arrived. Inside it sat a man with a clean overcoat and an expensive watch, someone who had not come to investigate a fire.

The man looked directly at the place where Batman had been standing.

Then he smiled into his telephone.

# 3. Two Men and a Towel

At eleven in the morning Reacher visited the old ferry station and found the doors welded shut.

Someone had done the welding recently. Bright metal showed under the rain. Two cameras watched the approach. A municipal sign promised the building would become a Community Resilience Center, which sounded to Reacher like a way to charge taxpayers twice for paint.

He walked around the block. There was a security gate, a fenced equipment yard, and a maintenance office with an empty desk. The desk held a coffee ring and a receipt from a place called Barlow's Hardware. On the back was a date three days before Mara disappeared.

Reacher did not touch it. There was a difference between seeing evidence and making evidence harder to use.

He found Barlow's four streets away. The proprietor had a hearing aid, an old Marine tattoo, and a mind like an inventory ledger.

"A woman? Short hair?" Barlow said. "Bought a spool of copper wire and three padlocks. Cash. Asked if I carried ear protection."

"You did?"

"Of course I did. She bought the best. Said the sound was getting into her dreams."

"Anyone with her?"

"No. But a car followed her. Gray. Two men."

Reacher asked what model. Barlow supplied it, plus the license prefix and the driver's habit of holding his cigarette between the wrong fingers.

"You a cop?" Barlow asked.

"Used to be."

"Good. Cops now won't tell me why a city contractor needs military-grade hearing protection."

Reacher didn't think the equipment was military-grade, but he let that pass.

At the rooming house he found his door slightly open. He had left it shut. He had also placed a loose thread across the jamb. The thread hung intact but crooked.

Two people had opened the door and attempted to restore it.

Reacher went downstairs instead of entering. The building's service corridor ran behind a laundry room that smelled of bleach, wet plaster, and institutional regret. An industrial washer thumped like a prisoner trying to escape.

On a hook hung a clean white towel.

Reacher took it because it was there.

He waited with his back near the corridor's broadest section. There were three men this time: one came out of the stairwell, one through the laundry door, and the third appeared at the end of the hall. They wore work jackets that hadn't seen much work. One held an object inside his pocket, pointed forward with the unnatural care of a man trying not to reveal a weapon.

"Mr. Reacher," the first one said. "We need to talk."

"You could have left a note."

"Come quietly."

"No."

The first man glanced at the others. They had planned for bargaining, not a refusal.

The confrontation lasted less than twenty seconds and seemed much longer to everyone except Reacher. Somebody swung too early. Somebody else slipped on detergent. The towel became a tangle of cloth and hands, useful mostly because it distracted the man who had been certain the big stranger would rush toward the visible threat. There were hard impacts, a broken mop handle, and one very expensive wristwatch skidding beneath a dryer.

When it ended, three men occupied the floor in various arrangements they hadn't chosen.

Reacher picked up the wristwatch. A message glowed on its face: *PIKE: Did he arrive?*

He put the watch in his pocket. He had paid for the room, and the men had damaged the door. He considered that an acceptable partial settlement.

One of them coughed.

"Who sent you?" Reacher asked.

The man stared at him.

"Bad answer."

"Pike," the man managed. "Gideon Pike."

"Where is Mara Rusk?"

"I don't know."

Reacher believed him. A lie usually came with a little too much detail. This man's terror had simplified his vocabulary.

"Where is Pike?"

"He goes to the night market. Freight district. He sells invitations."

"To what?"

The man swallowed. "The demonstration."

Reacher looked down at the towel. It had a torn seam and a bloodstain that hadn't been there before. He folded it neatly and put it in a laundry hamper.

"You picked a poor job," he told the men.

"You're gonna kill us?" the nearest asked.

"No. You're going to call an ambulance. Then you're going to find a new job."

"What if Pike comes for us?"

"Tell him I said hello."

Reacher took the stairs two at a time, returned to his room, retrieved the Barlow's address he had written on his receipt, and left through the fire escape.

At the bottom he nearly stepped on a small brown envelope wedged under the iron steps. It had his name printed in block capitals. No stamp. The inside smelled faintly of seawater.

There was one photograph. It showed Mara Rusk beside a technician in orange overalls, both wearing protective ear cups. Behind them rose an old brass bell, absurdly large, bolted into a concrete cradle. The bell's lip was lined with black material that absorbed the flash.

On the back she had written:

*If anyone comes asking for me, ask why the city needs a bell it can't afford to ring.*

Below that, in different handwriting:

*The Bat is not your friend.*

Reacher held the photograph at an angle. Two hands had written on it. The second writer pressed much harder. The ink had cut small furrows into the paper.

He had no idea who the Bat was. Gotham was full of people with strange titles, most of them elected.

A black car passed the mouth of the alley. Same make Barlow had described. Too dark to see the faces.

Reacher folded the photograph into his pocket and started toward the freight district.

He had come to find one missing woman. Apparently someone had turned that into a matter of considerable professional interest.

He disliked being popular.

# 4. A Rule About Masks

The freight district was a city that had died without anyone bothering to bury it.

Rail tracks disappeared under weeds. Cranes stood with their arms out over black water. The enormous windows of disused factories glowed here and there with the suspicious blue light of people who had no business being inside.

Reacher found the night market in the old fish exchange. It was not open yet. A line of expensive cars waited behind a chain-link fence. Men in black suits checked invitations through tinted windows. Above the roof, a broken sign still promised FRESH DAILY.

He decided not to introduce himself at the front gate.

Two blocks away, behind Pier Nineteen, he found a delivery truck idling without a driver. A gray van sat beside it. Its rear door carried the same white emblem he'd seen outside the diner.

Through the warehouse's open loading door, four men maneuvered a container onto a platform. The container was the size of a coffin and looked much heavier. Cables ran from it into a battered municipal siren mounted on a wheeled base.

A fifth man stood nearby, wearing a reflective safety vest. He was older and had a limp. He kept looking toward the container as though he had heard someone calling his name.

Reacher stepped into the shadow of a forklift. The men had guns. Two were impatient. One was frightened. He could usually tell which was which by how they stood when nobody spoke.

The frightened man reached for the container's latch.

A low note flowed across the pier.

Reacher's skin prickled. Suddenly the place seemed narrower. The iron beams leaned inward. For half a second he felt the heat of the old burning vehicle again, close enough to smell melting upholstery.

Then he saw the container opening, and the feeling broke.

The older workman staggered backward. One of the gunmen laughed and pushed him toward it. The workman fell to his knees.

A shape descended from the dark rafters.

It landed without a shout or announcement. Black armor, black cape, pointed ears. The first gunman fell as though the floor had been taken out from under him. A second fired wildly at the ceiling. The stranger moved through the muzzle flashes like something the eye refused to keep still.

Reacher had encountered military equipment that cost more than a small town. He had never encountered equipment that looked offended by the laws of gravity.

He moved toward the workman.

A cable snapped overhead. Its sparks scattered against the loading platform. The black figure spun, saw Reacher reach the container, and came at him with obvious intent.

"Step away," a voice said from behind a mask.

"Busy."

"Now."

Reacher caught the older man's collar and hauled him clear. The man clutched his chest, gasping, eyes unfocused.

The armored stranger closed the distance. Reacher saw the shift in his shoulders before he saw the strike. There was a tremendous impact, a shuffle of boots, and a brief, astonishing discovery on both sides: each had expected the other to move more than he did.

Batman looked at the large man whose hands were still holding the workman upright.

Reacher looked at the armored man whose forearm had left a deep ache across his ribs.

"You with them?" Batman demanded.

"That's a stupid question."

"Answer it."

"I'm the one moving the injured guy away from the machine."

Batman looked at the workman. He made a decision Reacher respected immediately: he stepped aside.

A gunman appeared behind the opened container. Batman turned and hurled something small. The man's weapon clattered away; the man himself followed it in a heap.

The siren emitted a second note.

Both men went rigid. Reacher saw rain falling upward. Batman heard a voice from another night, another failure, whispering that the city could never be saved because its protector enjoyed being needed.

Neither believed what he was experiencing. Neither could quite ignore it.

"Don't let it sound again," Batman said.

"Wasn't planning to."

Reacher dragged the workman toward open air. Batman reached the container and forced the lid closed around the dark ring inside. The sound ceased. An awful pressure left the room.

By the time Reacher had lowered the workman into the cab of a parked utility truck, three of the gunmen had fled and one was struggling to remember his name.

Batman emerged holding the sealed container with one hand. He was breathing harder than before.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Reacher."

"First name?"

"No."

A slight change in Batman's posture suggested that the answer had not improved his mood.

"Why are you here?"

"Looking for Mara Rusk."

That got a reaction. A tiny one. Reacher had watched faces for a living. The mask concealed most of this man's, but it didn't conceal the stillness.

"She's involved in something dangerous," Batman said.

"She vanished."

"That doesn't mean she was taken."

"I didn't say it did."

Batman studied him. Rain ran off the high edges of the mask. Up close, the armor showed repairs, scratches, old damage. Not a costume made for a party. A tool repaired by someone who kept choosing to use it.

"Gotham isn't a good place for private investigations," Batman said.

"Seems like the public ones aren't going too well either."

The workman groaned. Reacher checked that he was breathing steadily and leaned out of the cab.

"You got medical help coming?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Then quit threatening me and send them here."

Batman pressed a control on his wrist. Somewhere, a police dispatch channel changed tone. He watched Reacher for another long moment.

"Stay out of the Bellhouse," Batman said.

"Interesting. That's the second time someone's mentioned a place I didn't ask about."

"You don't know what you're dealing with."

"Then tell me."

Batman said nothing.

"That's what I thought," Reacher replied.

A siren sounded in the distance, ordinary this time. The black figure fired a line into the darkness and vanished upward.

Reacher waited until the workman's breathing normalized, then checked the loading platform. The fleeing men had dropped a leather notebook. In it were dates, weights, prices, and one penciled phrase:

*One city. Three notes. No witnesses.*

He tore out the page. He left the notebook for the police, because the rest of it belonged to people who might need it later.

Walking out through the rain, Reacher remembered the second handwriting on Mara's photograph.

*The Bat is not your friend.*

Maybe. But Reacher had seen him stop a fight to make room for an injured stranger.

He'd also seen him arrive like a missile aimed at the wrong target.

The jury, Reacher decided, could remain out.

# 5. The Names on the Form

Detective Renee Montoya hated paperwork for two reasons. One was that Gotham generated so much of it. The other was that the most important papers had a habit of disappearing.

The missing-person report for Mara Rusk was missing.

Not merely misplaced. Someone had replaced the first page with an amended version and deleted the camera still attached to the original. The new report said Mara had voluntarily departed for Metropolis. The signature belonged to a patrol sergeant who had been on leave that week.

Montoya was reading the report when a very large man appeared at her desk carrying a coffee cup.

"You Montoya?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Reacher."

"You're taller than the photographs."

He looked at her. "You have photographs?"

"Pier Nineteen. Half the security cameras went dark, but one caught a man your size entering just before a lot of expensive equipment got broken."

"Did it catch the other guy?"

"What other guy?"

"Big. Black suit. Bad attitude."

Montoya took a sip of cold coffee. "Welcome to Gotham."

She waved him into the chair opposite her desk. It made a noise like a chair filing a complaint.

"You knew Mara's father," she said.

"Yes."

"You're here because of an obligation?"

"An old one."

"That usually means somebody's going to get hurt."

"People already have."

He described the wounded dockworker, the container, the strange sound, and the men who had tried to collect him at his room. He did not mention the photograph's warning. Some details were more useful when you knew who was listening.

Montoya did not interrupt. When he finished, she brought up the name Gideon Pike.

There were no open warrants. There were seventeen complaints, six sealed settlements, and a photograph of Pike leaving a fundraiser with a senior police administrator. Officially he was a logistics consultant. Unofficially he moved city contracts between men who wanted money and men who wanted silence.

"He works for Intergang," Montoya said.

"I noticed the enthusiasm."

"You understand who they are?"

"A criminal organization with a good printing budget."

"Bruno Mannheim runs the eastern operation. He can buy a port authority, a police union, and the auction where they sell the old port authority. And lately he's been buying sound equipment."

"Why?"

"That's the question."

Reacher slid Mara's photograph onto the desk. Montoya looked at the bell, the protective ear cups, and the black material around the rim.

"Where'd you get this?"

"Under my stairs."

"Who put it there?"

"Someone who knew where I was staying."

"You aren't bothered by that?"

"I'm bothered by whoever opened my door first."

Montoya read both messages. Her expression changed at the second.

"The Bat is not your friend," she said. "You're getting involved in local politics."

"He's not a politician."

"Neither are most of the people who run this city."

"Could be forged."

"Could be. The document has a witness and time stamp. And her bank account received a very large payment."

Reacher considered the information without changing expression.

"Did the money move afterward?"

Montoya frowned. "Most of it. To a medical account for her mother."

June Rusk had said nothing about medical bills. That meant she had either forgotten, been embarrassed, or been warned. Reacher disliked all three possibilities.

"Mara could have signed and still been threatened," he said.

"Yes. She could have signed because she wanted the money. She could have discovered what they were doing and changed her mind. And she could have been helping all along. I don't get to pick before I investigate."

He liked Montoya more than he had expected.

"Then investigate," he said.

"I am. But my lieutenant has informed me this case is no longer mine."

"You going to listen?"

"No."

Reacher stood. He had learned everything she could safely tell him in a police station with too many listening devices.

At the door she called after him.

"Reacher. You're not going to kill Gideon Pike, are you?"

"Haven't met him."

"That's not an answer."

"Best one I've got."

She found the missing patrol sergeant's home number. The man answered on the second ring.

"Rusk?" she asked. "You signed a departure report for her?"

"I never heard of her," he replied.

Montoya looked at the amended page on her desk.

"Someone has," she said.

After the call, she reached under her chair and found a sealed envelope taped there that hadn't been present at the start of her shift.

*If the big man reaches the girl first, the city survives. If the Bat does, she doesn't.*

Montoya read it twice.

Then she burned the note in the ashtray she kept despite having quit smoking six years earlier.

Gotham was trying to tell a story about two men it barely knew.

She wasn't inclined to believe the narrator.

# 6. Invitations to the End of the World

Gideon Pike looked like a banker who had discovered crime paid in cash and required less conversation.

He had the smooth face of a man careful about what he ate and careless about what other people swallowed. At the old fish exchange, he greeted arriving buyers under chandeliers suspended from the beams where fish once hung.

The room smelled of incense, expensive cologne, and old salt trapped in the concrete.

A private auction of maritime infrastructure should not have included armed guards or women in evening clothes carrying cases handcuffed to their wrists. Reacher stood by a side entrance and watched Pike greet a man in a diplomatic suit. Two other guests arrived with security people wearing identical earpieces. There were no names on the invitations, only marks and numbers.

Reacher had no invitation. He had a black coat obtained at a charity shop for twelve dollars and an old Army posture nobody had ever successfully sold to a civilian.

A guard at the service door said, "Staff only."

"Good," Reacher said. "I'm looking for the kitchen."

"You got a badge?"

"No."

"Then leave."

Reacher leaned closer, so the guard could hear him without raising his voice. "Tell Gideon Pike I brought the missing photograph."

The guard's expression flickered. Reacher saw it and waited.

Five minutes later, Pike himself came to the door with the smile of a man making a temporary adjustment to an unpleasant situation.

"Mr. Reacher. I understand you've been looking into our employee."

"Mara Rusk."

"A troubled woman. Brilliant, but impulsive."

"Where is she?"

"Safe."

"You could be more specific."

"You are standing in a private venue. You are trespassing. I could call the police."

"You could."

Pike waited for Reacher to look nervous. Reacher waited for Pike to continue. Neither was disappointed by the other's persistence.

# 19. The Last Drink

Reacher left Gotham the following night.

He had a bus ticket, a clean shirt, and a new toothbrush. He had also acquired a small bruise behind one ear that reminded him not to take armored men at face value. He expected it would fade before his opinion did.

There were two hours before departure. He spent the first in a bar with faded photographs of prizefighters and a bartender who knew how to leave people alone.

He ordered a beer.

It was cold, good, and served in a thick glass mug that looked as though it had been made to survive arguments. Reacher drank half and watched a baseball game on the television. The sound was off. He could tell what had happened by the shape of the pitcher's shoulders.

A man sat beside him wearing an ordinary dark coat. Tall, broad through the chest, a little older than Reacher, with a healing cut near one hand and the unhurried posture of someone who normally traveled with people who opened doors for him.

"You're leaving," the man said.

"That's the general idea of a bus ticket."

The bartender approached. The stranger ordered sparkling water, then hesitated at Reacher's mug.

"Good beer?" he asked.

"Better than most."

"I'll have one."

The bartender left.

Reacher looked at the man's hand. There was a faint line of bruising across the knuckles. The suit under the coat was expensive enough to pay a year's rent on the room Reacher had rented. The man's voice had a scratch along its lower register that Reacher recognized despite the absence of electronic distortion.

"Wayne," Reacher said.

Bruce Wayne turned slightly. "Do we know each other?"

"Seen your photograph on things people want money for."

"Occupational hazard."

They watched the silent baseball game. A batter struck out and argued with the umpire. Reacher approved of neither man's position.

"I hear the city had a difficult evening," Wayne said.

"So I heard."

"Some people did very brave things."

"Some people did the job they were supposed to do."

"And others?"

"Needed a reason to start."

The bartender brought the second beer. Wayne held it by the handle and studied the level as if unfamiliar with a drink he couldn't improve by acquiring the company that made it.

"What happened to Mara Rusk?" he asked.

Reacher looked at him for a moment. "She's telling her story to people who can write it down."

"Good."

"You know her?"

"The Wayne Foundation has offered legal and medical support to people affected by the Bellhouse incident. No conditions."

"She'll appreciate that."

Wayne took a careful drink. "You don't trust rich men."

"Depends what they're doing with their money."

"And masked ones?"

Reacher kept his face expressionless. The bartender moved past them, carrying an empty tray. A train rumbled somewhere beyond the back wall.

"Depends what they're doing with their fists," Reacher said.

Wayne smiled faintly and looked down at his beer.

A man entered the bar wearing an Intergang emblem on his jacket. He was young and drunk and apparently unaware of the kind of room he'd entered. His gaze passed over Reacher and Wayne, then continued toward the jukebox. He was looking for music, not a fight.

Reacher went back to his drink. Wayne did the same.

The young man chose a song and left a dollar on the counter. For the moment he was just a man in a bad jacket, trying to enjoy the last few minutes of a day that had gone badly.

"You didn't get up," Wayne observed.

"Didn't need to."

"Yesterday you might have."

"Yesterday he might have needed stopping."

Wayne considered that. "Fair."

They let the song play. The jukebox was old and its speaker had a rattle in the bass. It was nothing like the sound at the Bellhouse. Reacher realized he was listening to make sure. Then he stopped worrying about it and listened to the song instead.

Wayne spoke again.

"I suspect some people would call what happened at the harbor a victory."

"They can call it that."

"What would you call it?"

Reacher thought of four women leaving a hidden hospital, of Mara Rusk looking at the evidence of her choices, of a father holding his frightened son on the waterfront. He thought of David Rusk asking for a beer after dragging him out of a burning vehicle.

"An evening when fewer people got hurt than somebody intended," he said.

Wayne nodded. "That's not a bad definition."

Reacher finished his beer. Wayne was only halfway through his. The bus would leave soon.

"You should finish that," Reacher said.

"Why?"

"Don't waste a good beer."

Wayne lifted the mug, considered the advice, and drank.

When the bartender returned, both glasses were empty. Reacher paid for his. Wayne paid for his own, which Reacher had expected.

Outside, the rain had returned, thin and cold enough to make the streetlights look like moons in puddles.

"Will you come back?" Wayne asked.

"If somebody needs me."

"And if somebody tells you to stay away?"

Reacher looked at the quiet man in the expensive coat. There was a cut on his hand, a city behind his eyes, and a stubbornness Reacher had come to recognize.

"Then they better have a good reason," he said.

They parted without a handshake.

At the corner Reacher glanced back. Wayne was still standing under the bar's awning, watching the city. His shoulders were straight. For a moment he looked like a man waiting for a signal only he could see.

Then he turned and walked into the rain.

Reacher headed for the bus terminal. He had met an enemy in Gotham, and perhaps a friend. Some people qualified for both. The city was still full of questions, and he had no intention of staying long enough to answer all of them.

But if the dark figure on the rooftops ever needed somebody to hold a door open while the people inside got out, Reacher suspected he'd recognize the call.

He boarded the bus, found a seat with room for his legs, and watched Gotham slide past the window.

Behind him, somewhere among the towers, a pale symbol opened against the clouds.

A bat, this time.

Reacher looked at it until the bus turned west.

Then he closed his eyes.

For once, nobody was counting.
