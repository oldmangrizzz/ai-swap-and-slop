You're right. And **Alex Pretti is exactly why the distinction between authorized conduct and actual conduct matters.**

On January 24, 2026, Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old ICU nurse at the Minneapolis VA, was shot and killed by federal immigration-enforcement personnel. The shooters were identified as Customs and Border Protection officers, rather than ICE officers specifically.

Video investigations found that Pretti appeared to have been disarmed before officers opened fire. CBP subsequently acknowledged that two officers fired their weapons. <Cite refs={["turn690359search2","turn690359search6","turn690359search10"]}/>

And that exposes the weakness in the threat assessment:

**A legal restriction is not a physical barrier to an unlawful act.**

The August 14 opinion may describe a narrow arrest authority. That does not establish that personnel will remain within those limits, that force will be proportional, or that civilians outside the designated areas will be safe.

Version 7 acknowledges this in principle, but it needs to carry that recognition through the operational analysis.

There's another material omission. **Page 9 of Version 7 discusses threats to medical personnel but relies on a 2025 incident at Broadview. It doesn't incorporate Pretti's January 2026 killing.** That means the assessment overlooked a more consequential example within its own reporting window.

One precision: the publicly released death record confirms multiple gunshot wounds but does not specify a head wound. That uncertainty doesn't change the central finding. <Cite refs={["turn355376search0","turn355376search47"]}/>

And the accountability question remains active. Reporting published in late October 2026 documents Minnesota investigators' allegations that federal authorities stopped sharing evidence from the Pretti and Renee Good shootings. Federal lawyers defended their discretion to withhold those materials. <Cite ref="turn159374view0"/>

So the correction is substantial:

**The threat model must account for what armed personnel actually do, including actions outside their authority, not merely what their written orders permit.**

That means assessing observed excessive-force incidents, disputed accounts of encounters, failures to provide medical assistance, and impediments to independent investigation alongside troop movements and legal memoranda.

It does not establish that military personnel will repeat what happened to Pretti. It establishes that relying on legal limits alone to discount civilian exposure would be an analytical error.

And yes, that distinction should already have been reflected in Version 7.
